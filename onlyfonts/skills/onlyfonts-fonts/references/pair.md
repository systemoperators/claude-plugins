# Pairing typefaces

When to add a second face, how to choose it, and the free pairs with the best evidence. Numbers in brackets point to sources.md.

## When to pair at all

- One family first: use its weights, widths, italics, optical sizes, case and size before adding a face. In a 2026 sample of 2,190 award-gallery sites, 60% used one family for headline and body. (S10, S1, S44)
- Add a second face only for a job the first cannot do: code or data beside prose, asides, a missing weight or italic, a missing script, a brand voice. (S10, S33)
- Two faces for most work, a third rarely, four almost never. (S1, S21)
- A mono for code, numbers or labels is the cheapest second or third face; 23% of the designed sites used one, mostly for labels. (S44, S10)
- Build hierarchy with size, weight, color and space before reaching for a second face. (S21, S10, S1)

## Roles

- Give each face one role and keep it everywhere: headings, body, notes or data. Switch faces at a paragraph or element, never inside a sentence. (S1, S3, S6)
- Limit each face to a size range; a display face stays large and appears rarely. (S29)
- Make the body face the anchor and choose companions against it. (S6)
- Pairs run one way: A for headings over B for body is a different pair from B over A. Always say which face does what. (S43)

## One thing the same, one thing different

- Keep one trait shared (skeleton, proportions, x-height, mood or era) and make one trait clearly different (serif and sans, width, weight, role). (S29, S33)
- The failure is two faces close enough to look like a mistake: two similar geometric sans, two similar old-style serifs, or slightly different stroke endings. Make them plainly different or use one family. (S30, S31, S32, S10, S3)
- Match the skeleton, vary the flesh. Describe each face by its form model and its flesh. (S11, S30)
- Form models: dynamic (open apertures, slanted stress, from the pen), rational (closed apertures, vertical stress), geometric (built from circles and lines). Flesh: sans or serif, low or high contrast. (S11)
- Same form model with different flesh pairs well. Opposite corners make a strong, deliberate contrast. Same flesh with a different form model looks wrong. (S11)
- Compatible structure means similar x-height, extender length, width, aperture and stroke contrast. A sibling shares most of these, a cousin two or three. (S6, S33, S10)
- Faces from one period or one tool share a story (an old-style serif with a humanist sans). A clash of eras should be on purpose. (S29, S31, S33, S4)
- Sometimes the only shared trait is mood; that is enough when the roles are clear. (S33, S29)

## How much contrast (disputed)

- Lupton asks for emphatic contrast over harmony. (S3)
- Butterick says any two clearly different faces can work, lower contrast can beat higher, and serif with sans is no law: newspapers pair two serifs. (S1)
- Santa Maria, quoted in the Google lesson, asks for a balance of distinction and harmony, and a serif with a sans when in doubt. (S10)
- Our rule: serif with sans as the default; two faces of one class only when their roles are clear and they differ plainly. (S1, S10)
- Serif headline over sans body is the safe pattern and now also the one most likely to look like a template: 35% of paired designed sites used it, and 36% used sans over sans. (S44)
- Hierarchy can be extreme and still read as intent: weights far apart, headlines three to four times the body size, tight tracking on large type. Designed sites in 2026 had a median 64 px headline over a 16 px body. (S40, S44)

## Making a pair sit well

- On one line, size the faces so their x-heights match; CSS font-size-adjust does this. (S3, S30, S18)
- On separate lines, add contrast in size and weight too: big and light over small and dark. (S3)
- Match optical size, not point size: text cut beside text cut, display cut for headlines. (S34)
- Judge a pair on the real content. Squint at a full page: each element's gray should differ enough from the body to read as a marker. (S6, S55)
- Shortcuts (superfamily, same designer) narrow the field; the content still decides. (S6)
- Non-experts barely notice a weak pair and experts do: experts picked the real pairing over a random swap 75% of the time, non-experts 60 to 65%. (S43)

## Seen in real work

Counts are small; read them as patterns, not rankings.

- On designed sites in 2026: Playfair Display, Fraunces or Instrument Serif headlines over an Inter body; Instrument Serif over DM Sans; Instrument Sans with Inter. (S44)
- With Inter, designers mostly add a serif with character for headings, or a mono (Space Mono, DM Mono) for labels. (S35)
- Real pairs are a long tail: 94% of pairs on designed sites appeared once, and the listicle classic Montserrat with Lora appeared on none of 2,190 sites. (S44)
- Pair lists online lean on popular fonts. 43% of web-recommended pairs used one of the five most popular fonts, against under 8% in pairs from 15 million pages of real documents. (S43)
- Hand-written pair lists in other skills carry no evidence of use, and one font tool copied such a list as its proven pairs. (S57)
- Fonts In Use lists faces per project but publishes no pair counts; the free families highest in its counts are Inter, Montserrat, Roboto, Poppins, Open Sans and Work Sans. (S45)
- So generate pairs from the rules above, with reasons, rather than reciting a list. (S43, S44)

## A mono beside text

- Choose the mono by form model too: under a dynamic serif, a dynamic mono matches; a geometric mono such as Space Mono contrasts on purpose. (S11)
- Check the mono for clear 0 O and 1 l I, and that it covers the text's languages. (S12)

## Reading get_font's measures

- get_font measures each family from its regular file. x_height and cap_height are a share of the em. width is the mean width of a to z as a share of the em, with width_class narrow, normal or wide. (OnlyFonts' own method)
- stroke_ratio is the thinnest stroke over the thickest, with contrast linear, low, medium or high. Where Google classifies the family, classification ("serif/transitional") and form_model (dynamic, rational, geometric) come too. (OnlyFonts' own method; contrast checked against Google's measured stroke widths)
- Faces that meet on one line: pick x-heights within about 0.03 of each other, or set the smaller with the font-size-adjust value get_font gives. (S3, S18, S30)
- Pick a partner that differs clearly in contrast or classification, not slightly: medium contrast beside low reads as a mistake, high beside linear reads as a choice. (S30, S31)
- Same form_model with different flesh pairs well (a dynamic serif with a dynamic sans); the same flesh with a different form_model looks wrong. (S11)
- For body text and UI, prefer contrast linear to medium and width_class normal; keep high contrast and narrow widths for headings. (S10, S35)
- A null measure means unknown, not absent: about 40% of families have no Google classification. Judge those from the specimen on the family's page. (OnlyFonts' own method)

## Reading get_font's pairings

- get_font lists a few pairings per family. They are computed, not observed. Points go to category contrast (serif with sans, a mono, a display face), the same superfamily, the same designer, and a partner with four or more styles. That score is blended with visual relatedness from an image model. There is no co-use data yet. (OnlyFonts' own method)
- The list gives no roles. Assign them by category: a display or handwriting partner sets headings, a mono sets code, a text face sets body. (S43)
- Treat each as a candidate. Open it with get_font and check styles for its role, languages for the text, the designer, and the same-or-different rule above. (S10, S30)
- Prefer a listed partner from the same superfamily or designer when there is one. (S10)
- get_font also lists look-alikes ("similar"). Use them to find an alternative to an overused face; never pair a face with one of its look-alikes. (S30, S31)
